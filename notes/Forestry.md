- [Counting individual trees from satellite imagery — TreeMatch and TinyTrees (ECCV 2026)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.24786): LinkedIn post by Loic Landrieu, 30 June 2026, on the ECCV 2026 paper *Counting Trees from Satellite Imagery with Noisy Supervision* (Gominski, Mugabowindekwe, Xu, Tong, Brandt, Le, Fensholt, Samaras, Landrieu). The case for counting: *"many applications, such as reforestation monitoring, biomass estimation, and biodiversity assessment, require reasoning at the tree level: detecting and counting individual trees."* The paper makes three contributions. TinyTrees is a tree-counting dataset covering 25,890 km² of China, Rwanda and France, from 3 sensors, with 216M individual trees. A loss based on unbalanced optimal transport handles the counting. A "marginal slack" training strategy mixes strong manual labels with weaker labels from ALS or ultra-high-resolution imagery. The resulting model, TreeMatch, *"accurately counts trees across diverse biomes"*. Code: [dgominski/treematch](https://github.com/dgominski/treematch), no license. Data: [dgominski/tinytrees](https://huggingface.co/datasets/dgominski/tinytrees) on Hugging Face. *Keywords: tree counting, TinyTrees, TreeMatch, optimal transport, noisy labels, ECCV 2026*
  - Source: [LinkedIn post](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/loic-landrieu-235b8533_eccv2026-share-7477660745120284674-N8tC); see [[LinkedIn]].
  - Counting rather than delineating crowns: compare SelvaBox's per-crown labels further down this page.

- [Semantic segmentation of forest stands using deep learning](https://www.silvafennica.fi/article/25010): LinkedIn post by SmartForest4.0 (Norway), 26 June 2026, on Sandum, Ørka, Tomic, Næsset & Gobakken, *Silva Fennica* 60(1), article 25010 (DOI 10.14214/sf.25010). The problem is that stand delineation *"is often done manually by experts interpreting aerial images — a process that can be time-consuming and sometimes subjective."* The authors trained a U-Net on a 5-layer input: multispectral aerial imagery (0.25 m, resampled to 1 m) plus a canopy height model from airborne laser scanning. 760 tiles were split 532 train / 114 validation / 114 test. Overall accuracy was 0.72, with 78–85% for non-forest and early-stage classes and 55–79% for mature ones. It works best in well-managed spruce stands and over-segments irregular stands. Methods and datasets are listed on [smartforest.no](https://smartforest.no/methods-and-datasets/). *Keywords: forest stand delineation, U-Net, ALS canopy height, forest inventory, Norway, Silva Fennica*
  - Source: [LinkedIn post](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/forestmanagement-ai-deeplearning-share-7476251856902643712-h0qr); see [[LinkedIn]].

- [Scaling Transparency: Annual, Pan-Tropical Commodity Maps Powered by AlphaEarth Foundations](https://medium.com/google-earth/scaling-transparency-annual-pan-tropical-commodity-maps-powered-by-alphaearth-foundations-5f4066b5dd13): Google Earth blog post by Alicia Sullivan and Katelyn Tarrio, 13 May 2026. It announces annual 10 m maps of coffee, cocoa, oil palm and rubber across the whole tropical belt. They are built on the 64-dimensional AlphaEarth Foundations Satellite Embedding instead of the earlier Sentinel-1/Sentinel-2/PALSAR-2 composites, and they are a core output of the Forest Data Partnership. The post claims *"the first pan-tropical, open source, 10-meter resolution map for cocoa, coffee, and rubber, key commodity tree crops associated with deforestation."* 2020 and 2024 are out now, with a backfill for every year 2017–2025 under way. CC BY 4.0, in the Earth Engine FDaP catalog and as COGs in a requester-pays GCS bucket. Training data came from OFI, Alliance Bioversity–CIAT and Meridia, and known errors remain in regrowth and agroforestry areas. The new model version is `model_2025b` in [google/forest-data-partnership](https://github.com/google/forest-data-partnership/tree/main/models/model_2025b). *Keywords: commodity crops, cocoa coffee rubber oil palm, AlphaEarth, Forest Data Partnership, EUDR, pan-tropical*

- [PhD position: Multi-temporal forest canopy height reconstruction from satellite data](https://jobs.uzh.ch/job-vacancies/phd-position-multi-temporal-forest-canopy-height-reconstruction-from-satellite-data/f49ee8f9-3652-4f0e-84ab-94c99e374da8): A University of Zurich PhD vacancy, jointly supervised by Prof. Livia Piermattei (Remote Sensing of Environmental Changes, Department of Geography) and Prof. Jan Dirk Wegner (EcoVision Lab, Department of Mathematical Modeling and Machine Learning). - [Comparing deforestation data for South American soy](https://landcarbonlab.org/insights/data-comparison-deforestation-soy-south-america/): LinkedIn post by Land & Carbon Lab, 23 July 2026, on why three widely used deforestation datasets give different answers for soy supply chains: *"Subtle differences in what each one measures, and how it defines 'forest,' can lead to varying answers."* The linked article by Elise Mazur, Benjamin Wielgosz and Ana Beatriz dos Santos (20 July 2026) compares UMD/Global Forest Watch Tree Cover Loss, MapBiomas land cover transitions and PRODES across Brazil, Argentina and Bolivia. The three disagree because they define forest differently: TCL counts any tree cover over 5 m tall, MapBiomas uses country-specific ecological definitions, and PRODES records human-driven loss of native vegetation regardless of height. Filtering MapBiomas to the FAO forest definition cuts its forest loss by up to 55% in Argentina but barely changes it in Bolivia. Soy-linked deforestation is only 3–12% of total forest loss, depending on country and dataset. The datasets agree on continental hotspots but diverge in regional totals. The advice is to pick the dataset that fits the use, or to compare several independent sources (a "convergence of evidence" approach). The method for aligning local data with AFi, SBTN and FAO definitions is in the WRI working paper [Comparing Forest Loss Data Sources to Align Deforestation- and Conversion-Free Analyses for Soy Production in South America](https://www.wri.org/research/forest-loss-data-sources-deforestation-conversion-free-analyses-soy-production-latin-america) (DOI [10.46830/wriwp.25.00003](https://doi.org/10.46830/wriwp.25.00003)). *Keywords: deforestation data, soy supply chains, forest definitions, PRODES, MapBiomas, Tree Cover Loss*
  - Source: [LinkedIn post](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/deforestation-soy-supplychains-share-7486066279666089984-qmoq); see [[LinkedIn]]. Both `lnkd.in` links were resolved and their `utm_medium` tracking was stripped.
  - Directly relevant to the EUDR tools on this page, WAC and the Forest Data Partnership models: which forest definition you choose changes what counts as deforestation.

- [WAC — World AgroCommodities](https://github.com/masolele/WAC): *WAC — World AgroCommodities: Earth Observation for Deforestation-Free Supply Chains.* Code, models and utilities from the World AgroCommodities project, which supports the EU Deforestation Regulation (EUDR) with satellite data. The project aims to map commodity parcels and crop types over demonstration sites, detect deforestation and land conversion after the EUDR's December 2020 cut-off, and give EU Member States open, reproducible methods built on openEO and the Copernicus Data Space Ecosystem (CDSE). It also validates the algorithms and supports national uptake. The repository is a Python package (`world_agrocommodities`, managed with `uv`) with optional notebook dependencies. *Keywords: EUDR, deforestation-free supply chains, commodity mapping, openEO, CDSE, land conversion*
  - Pairs with Google's Forest Data Partnership commodity models further down this page: both aim at EUDR compliance, but WAC runs on the European openEO/CDSE stack rather than Earth Engine.

- [NDVI Tree Crown Detection](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/amir-sharifi-313a0160_remotesensing-python-computervision-share-7490188575767154688-zCgy/): LinkedIn post by Amir Sharifi open-sourcing *"a small desktop tool I used couple of years ago for labeling part of my research"* that builds tree crown masks from RGB-NIR imagery. The pipeline is deliberately classical, not learned: load a 4-band (RGB + NIR) TIFF, compute NDVI to find vegetation, clean the mask with morphological operations, then separate touching crowns with a distance transform and watershed segmentation, preview the boundaries, and export a binary PNG mask for use in deep learning datasets. The GUI exposes the NDVI threshold and segmentation parameters, organises processed images, and lets you flag hard cases for manual labelling. He is explicit about scope: *"This isn't intended to replace manual annotation. Instead, it generates a reasonable initial mask that can reduce the amount of time researchers spend labeling tree crowns from scratch."* Repository: [amirsharifi97/NDVI-Tree-Crown-Detection](https://github.com/amirsharifi97/NDVI-Tree-Crown-Detection) — Python, no license declared, last pushed August 2024. *Keywords: tree crown segmentation, NDVI threshold, watershed, pre-annotation, mask generation, RGB-NIR*
  - Pre-annotation rather than detection: it sits upstream of the learned tree crown detectors on this page, such as SelvaBox, by cheapening the labels they need. The same watershed-after-threshold recipe appears in the scikit-image thresholding note on [[Python]].
  - No license is declared on the repository, so it is default copyright — see the licensing watch on [[Code_Repositories]].

- [SelvaBox: A high-resolution dataset for tropical tree crown detection](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.00170): SelvaBox: A high-resolution dataset for tropical tree crown detection. - [Forest Data Partnership on Earth Engine](https://developers.google.com/earth-engine/datasets/publisher/forestdatapartnership): Forest Data Partnership. Publisher page for datasets supporting "global monitoring of commodity-driven deforestation, forest degradation and restoration efforts". Includes per-pixel commodity probability models for cocoa, coffee, palm and rubber at 10 m (2025a and 2025b versions), and Forest Persistence v0, a 30 m score in [0, 1] for whether a pixel held undisturbed forest in 2020, built by convergence of evidence across sources. Several versions are tagged for EUDR compliance; the datasets are not yet peer-reviewed. *Keywords: deforestation, EUDR, commodity mapping, forest persistence, Google Earth Engine, cocoa palm rubber coffee*
